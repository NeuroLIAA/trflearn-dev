    Recommendations¶

    TRFlearn leaves several choices to you: how to choose λ, how to validate, how to preprocess, which penalty to use. This page says what to choose in which case, and why. The why is theory wherever theory decides; where a choice depends on a crossover theory cannot place, the numbers come from simulations in which the true response is known:

    • two predictors fitted together, a speech-like envelope and a sparse onset train, at −20, −10 and 0 dB, with 2.5 to 30 minutes of data, white or 1/f noise, single subjects and cohorts of 16;
    • one spectrogram-like predictor of 1 to 32 bands whose bands correlate at 0, 0.5 or 0.9.

    At −20 dB the held-out correlation is about 0.06, the range of speech-envelope TRFs in EEG. In the first set the onsets carry under 1 % of the response's variance: a weak predictor, which matters below.

    At a glance¶

    If the result is… Choose λ on Solver Preprocessing
    The TRF itself: its shape, amplitude, group average R² ridge Standardize
    Prediction accuracy (encoding or decoding r) Pearson ridge, or a banded ridge when one group of predictors is much weaker Standardize
    A weak predictor's TRF in one subject at EEG-like SNR Pearson ridge Standardize

    In every case:

    • search λ: gridsearchCV() with no grid sweeps np.logspace(-3, 3, 13) and chooses on R²; pass metric="Pearson" when prediction is the result, and extend the grid when TRFlearn warns that the choice is on its edge;
    • validate with create_partitions(), which makes 5 folds, stratified by segment when the data come in segments;
    • leave metric_limit_percentage=None;
    • fit long recordings in float32 on the GPU;
    • fit a cohort with TRFGroup, with one λ for every subject (regularization_strategy="mean-curve") when subjects are compared, and compare them in original units, which is how TRFGroup hands its kernels out.

    Choosing λ¶

    What λ means here¶

    TRFlearn solves the normal equations averaged per sample,

    \[ \Big(\tfrac{1}{n} X^\top X + \lambda M\Big)\, w = \tfrac{1}{n} X^\top y , \]

    where \(n\) is the number of samples and \(M = I\) for ridge. With Standardize every column of \(X\) has unit variance, so the diagonal of \(\tfrac{1}{n} X^\top X\) is 1: λ is measured against the variance of one predictor, and λ = 1 penalizes a coefficient as much as one standard deviation of its predictor weighs in the fit. Because of the \(1/n\), an λ means the same with 2 or with 30 minutes of data. The best λ still moves, for the reasons below.

    Which λ to expect¶

    Ridge is the posterior mean of a Bayesian model in which the noise has variance \(\sigma^2\) and every coefficient is drawn independently with variance \(\tau^2\). That posterior mean solves the equations above with

    \[ \lambda^\star = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\,\tau^2}. \]

    With standardized predictors and target, the variance the response gets from the predictors is \(S = \tau^2 \operatorname{tr}(C)\), where \(C = \tfrac{1}{n} X^\top X\) is the correlation matrix of the lagged predictors. Its diagonal is 1, so \(\operatorname{tr}(C) = p\), the number of coefficients (predictors × lags), whatever the correlations between predictors. Writing \(\mathrm{SNR} = S / \sigma^2\):

    \[ \lambda^\star = \frac{p}{n \cdot \mathrm{SNR}} = \frac{n_\text{predictors} \cdot n_\text{lags}}{n_\text{samples} \cdot \mathrm{SNR}} . \]

    Four things move it:

    1. Noise. \(\lambda^\star \propto 1/\mathrm{SNR}\): one decade more for every 10 dB less. The noisier the data, the more of what the fit sees is noise, and the harder the prior has to pull.
    2. Data length. \(\lambda^\star \propto 1/n\): one decade less for every tenfold more data. The more data, the less the estimate needs the prior.
    3. Number of coefficients. \(\lambda^\star \propto p\). Spread the same response over more coefficients and each one carries less (\(\tau^2 = S/p\)), so the prior should pull harder: a 32-band spectrogram needs a larger λ than one envelope, and so does a longer window. The sample rate cancels out: doubling it doubles both the lags and the samples.
    4. Collinearity. It does not enter \(\lambda^\star\), since \(\operatorname{tr}(C) = p\) regardless, but it decides what the data can tell apart. Write \(C = \sum_i \mu_i v_i v_i^\top\). Along each direction \(v_i\), ridge keeps a fraction \(\mu_i / (\mu_i + \lambda)\) of the estimate. Correlated predictors put most of their variance in a few directions with large \(\mu_i\) — what they share — and leave many with small \(\mu_i\) — how they differ —, where noise is amplified by \(1/\mu_i\) unless λ suppresses it. A response the correlated predictors share lives in the large directions, so a larger λ costs it little and cross-validation picks one. A response in how they differ lives in the small directions, and no λ recovers it.

    In simulation, λ chosen on R² follows all four directions: per decade of predictors it grows by 1.0 to 1.4 decades, per decade of data it falls by 0.6 to 1.4, and per 10 dB of SNR it falls by 0.5 to 1.2. It sits about one decade above \(\lambda^\star\), since real kernels are smooth and concentrated rather than independent coefficients. Correlated bands (0.5 to 0.9) add another 0.5 to 1.5 decades from four bands up. And with bands correlated at 0.9, a response carried by one band, or a different one by each band, comes out with a kernel error of 0.6 to 0.9 at 8 to 32 bands, against 0.3 to 0.5 when the bands share it.

    The grid¶

    • np.logspace(-3, 3, 13), in standardized units, held every choice with one or two predictors, and 95 % of them for up to 32 correlated bands (between \(10^{-1.5}\) and \(10^{2}\)). It is what gridsearchCV searches when given no grid.
    • A grid denser than two points per decade changes nothing, and costs about the same: λ is scored from sums, so each extra point is cheap.
    • With many more coefficients, or a much lower SNR, than that, extend the top: about one decade per tenfold more coefficients or 10 dB less SNR.
    • TRFlearn warns when the selected λ is the smallest or the largest of the grid: the search stopped there instead of at a peak. At the top, extend upwards — or accept that the predictor is being shrunk away. At the bottom, extend downwards — or the metric curve is flat because the predictors explain almost nothing; the validation plot tells which.
    • Do not fix λ across datasets. In simulation λ = 1 matches the search at −10 and 0 dB and loses at −20 dB; 0.1 and 10 each lose badly somewhere.

    Pearson, R² or RMSE¶

    Pearson's correlation is blind to the scale of a prediction:

    \[ r(a\,\hat y,\, y) = r(\hat y,\, y) \qquad \text{for any } a > 0 . \]

    As λ grows, ridge mostly shrinks the prediction, and until its shape changes Pearson does not notice: the curve is flat near its peak and the peak sits at a larger λ. R² charges for the shrinkage,

    \[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (y_t - \hat y_t)^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \bar y)^2}, \]

    since a prediction that is too small leaves residual. It selects the λ with the smallest held-out squared error, the loss ridge itself minimizes. RMSE is a monotone transform of the same error and selects the same λ.

    • The kernels are the result: R². It chooses about half a decade less λ. In simulation it recovers 0.85 of the envelope's amplitude against 0.66, and the 16-subject group average is better for both predictors: averaging removes noise, not shrinkage.
    • A weak predictor in one subject at EEG-like SNR: Pearson. There the extra shrinkage keeps the estimate from being mostly noise. With R², the weak onsets' kernel error at −20 dB rose by 0.95.
    • Prediction is the result: Pearson, which keeps a small edge of 0.001 to 0.005 in held-out r.

    To collapse the folds' curves into one, keep the mean (the default) rather than the median (metric_aggregation_function).

    • The mean uses every fold at every λ. A fold spoiled by a bad segment has its whole curve scaled towards zero by one factor (for noise unrelated to the stimulus, in Pearson and in R² alike), which gives it less weight in the mean without dragging the peak elsewhere.
    • The median takes a different fold at different λ, as 'max' does, so its curve is rougher. It would only help against a fold whose curve has a different shape, which the simulations did not include.
    • In simulation, the median changed the λ chosen on Pearson for 38 % of subjects at −20 dB and 19 % at −10 dB. It improved no kernel; at −10 dB the envelope's error rose by 0.06 where λ changed. On R² it changed λ for 3–7 % of subjects, with no effect.

    metric_limit_percentage¶

    It selects the largest λ whose metric is within a fraction of the best. Because a Pearson curve is flat near its peak, 0.01 buys a much larger λ, and 0.05 runs to the top of the grid. In simulation the envelope kept 0.34 of its amplitude at 0.01 and 0.005 at 0.05. An R² curve is steeper: 0.01 changes almost nothing, and 0.05 moves λ about half a decade up, back to where Pearson chooses, with the envelope's shrinkage that comes with it. The shape does not improve either: even at −20 dB with 2.5 minutes of data, the correlation between estimated and true kernel stays the same. Leave it None when the kernels are the result.

    To decide from a pilot whether a response is there, test the held-out r against a permutation null and check that the kernel's shape is stable across folds or subjects; a more shrunken kernel looks smoother but carries no more information.

    Validation¶

    What is recovered barely depends on the scheme. pooled and averaged, 3, 5 or 10 folds, stratified or contiguous, leave-one-segment-out, an inner split of 60 or 80 %: they choose the same λ and return the same kernels. The differences are elsewhere:

    • train_test loses. Its final fit sees 80 % of the data, and its kernels show it. Use it for quick looks.
    • Cost (relative to pooled, 5 folds): averaged 0.4–0.5×, 3 folds 0.6×, 10 folds 2.0×. Leave-one-segment-out costs as many folds as there are segments: the same as 5 folds with 5 segments, 6× with 30.
    • Stability. averaged chooses the same λ for more subjects.
    • Honesty. The cross-validated score is a fair estimate of the score on unseen data: in simulation they differ by at most 0.007 in r.

    Recommended: create_partitions(), the default, which makes 5 folds stratified by segment (plain contiguous folds for one continuous recording), or n_folds=3 when time matters. With many short trials, keep stratified k-fold rather than leave-one-segment-out: same result, a fraction of the cost.

    From validation to the kernel you show¶

    Cross-validation does two jobs, and the kernel you show is a third.

    1. Measuring predictive power. Report the cross-validated score: the metric on data that neither the fit nor the choice of λ saw, which pooled guarantees with its inner split. Never report the score of a fit on all the data: it is measured on the data it was fitted to, and is inflated. To ask whether a predictor adds anything, compare the two models' cross-validated scores on the same folds.
    2. Choosing λ. The validation curve's peak, on the metric that suits the question (see Pearson, R² or RMSE).
    3. Estimating the kernel. Fit with all the data at the chosen λ. This is not overfitting: λ is what controls overfitting, and holding data back would only make the kernel noisier. In TRFlearn, fit(regularization) stores in coef_ the average of the k folds' kernels, each fitted on \((k-1)/k\) of the data; with 5 folds it differs from a single fit on all of it by about 0.5 %.

    One λ for a whole group¶

    When every subject hears the same stimulus, they share the lagged design \(X\) and \(C = \tfrac{1}{n} X^\top X\). For subject \(s\), with response \(w_s\) and noise \(\varepsilon_s\),

    \[ \hat w_s(\lambda) = (C + \lambda I)^{-1}\, \tfrac{1}{n} X^\top y_s , \qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\hat w_s(\lambda)\big] = (C + \lambda I)^{-1} C\, w_s \equiv A_\lambda w_s . \]

    The noise only adds variance; the expected kernel is the true one passed through an operator \(A_\lambda\) that depends on the stimulus and on λ, and on nothing about the subject.

    With one λ for everyone, every subject's kernel goes through the same \(A_\lambda\), so anything a group analysis computes as a weighted sum of kernels keeps its expectation:

    \[ \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_s c_s\, \hat w_s(\lambda)\Big] = A_\lambda \sum_s c_s\, w_s , \]

    whether it is a group mean (\(c_s = 1/S\)), a difference between groups or a slope against a covariate. Two groups with the same response have the same expected kernel, whatever their noise; responses in a ratio of 0.8 keep that ratio.

    With one λ per subject, \(\mathbb{E}[\hat w_s] = A_{\lambda_s} w_s\), and \(\lambda_s\) is not arbitrary: as above, the best λ grows with the subject's noise and falls with the size of its response. A noisier subject's kernel is shrunk more for the same response, a stronger responder's less: data quality turns into TRF amplitude, and real differences between subjects are exaggerated.

    With different stimuli of the same kind (different stories, one speaker) \(C\) differs little between subjects and the argument holds approximately; that case was not simulated.

    In simulation. Two groups of 12 subjects who hear one stimulus, 5 minutes each, at −20 and −10 dB, 60 cohorts per condition. The test is Welch's t on the envelope's peak (the eight channels and ±16 ms where the true response peaks), on kernels in original units:

    λ Same response, noise 6 dB apart: p < 0.05 Mean t True ratio 0.8, estimated Power
    One for everyone, from the mean validation curve 9 % +0.04 0.80 82 %
    One per subject, on R² 54 % −2.5 0.75 77 %
    One per subject, on Pearson 8 % −0.5 0.78 42 %
    One per group, from each group's mean curve, on R² 71 % −5.1 0.78 85 %
    • With one λ the t statistics follow their null distribution (Kolmogorov–Smirnov p = 0.5). The 9 % is 11 cohorts of 120, which a test at 5 % produces by chance with p = 0.05.
    • With an λ per subject on R², the noisier group's kernel came out 22 % smaller for the same response, and a true ratio of 0.8 read as 0.75.
    • Pearson's per-subject λ hides that by shrinking everyone a lot: fewer false positives than R², but half the power, and its t statistics are still off-centre.
    • An λ per group is the worst: it lines the shrinkage up with the very label being tested.

    Covariates. In cohorts of 16 whose gains spread over 0.7–1.3 and whose noise spreads over ±5 dB, regress log amplitude on log gain and on noise.

    • With one λ, the slopes are 1.00 and 0.00: the truth.
    • With an λ per subject on R² at −20 dB, they are 1.25–1.33 and −0.28 per 10 dB. Amplitude exaggerates real differences, and it loses about a quarter per 10 dB of noise. At −10 dB the distortion is much smaller (1.03–1.06 and −0.05).

    Which common λ. The tests are valid because the λ is shared, not because of where it sits, so any λ chosen without looking at the groups serves them.

    Where it sits does change the group-average kernel:

    • For the envelope and for weak onsets, the mean validation curve on R² matched the best λ in hindsight.
    • For a strong predictor, the average wants less shrinkage than one subject's fit, since averaging already cancels noise. Leave-one-subject-out finds it: take the λ at which the other subjects' average kernel best predicts each left-out subject. At −20 dB the group kernel of strong onsets had an error of 0.24 this way and 0.41 with the mean curve, against 0.23 at the best λ.

    So:

    • for comparisons and covariates, use one λ for every subject, from the mean validation curve on R², chosen without looking at the groups;
    • for the average shape of a strong predictor, choose that λ leave-one-subject-out;
    • for a single subject, keep that subject's own λ.

    In TRFGroup, regularization_strategy="mean-curve" shares the λ of the mean validation curve, in two passes: search_subject keeps each subject's curve, share_regularization picks the λ, and fit_subject fits everyone at it (see Group analysis). regularization_strategy="leave-one-out" chooses it leave-one-subject-out, in a single pass (see Leave-one-subject-out). Left unset, regularization_strategy fits each subject at its own λ and warns when they differ; regularization_strategy="subject" does the same without the warning, for an analysis that wants it.

    Compare subjects in original units¶

    Standardize divides each subject's recording by its own standard deviation \(s_y\), and \(s_y^2 = g^2 v + \sigma^2\) holds the noise \(\sigma^2\) as well as the response's variance \(g^2 v\). A kernel in standardized units is the kernel in the recording's units divided by \(s_y\), so a noisier subject's kernel is smaller even with one λ. These are the units the plots label \(\sigma_y/\sigma_x\).

    For a one-sample question — is there a response at all? — that is harmless: noise cannot create a mean. For comparing groups, or relating amplitude to a covariate, it is not. In the simulation above, standardized kernels told the two groups with the same response apart in 93–100 % of cohorts, whichever way λ was chosen. Use kernels in original units: the recording's unit per unit of each predictor. The model predicts \(\hat y = \mu_y + \sigma_y \sum_d w[d]\,(x[t-d] - \mu_x)/\sigma_x\), so \(w\,\sigma_y/\sigma_x\) is the same fitted model written in those units, and λ keeps its meaning. TRFGroup hands every kernel out that way by default (kernel_units="original"). For a single model, multiply coef_ by \(\sigma_y/\sigma_x\) from the training data, or fit with target_preprocess=None on recordings whose mean is zero (high-pass filtered, as EEG usually is).

    Neither unit is neutral. Data units keep everything that multiplies a recording, such as skull thickness or electrode placement; standardized units keep the noise level. With one λ, original units at least treat every subject with the same estimator.

    Preprocessing¶

    Standardize, and why centring matters¶

    TRFlearn fits no intercept, and centring the data is what stands in for one. For ridge with an unpenalized intercept \(b\),

    \[ \min_{b,\,w}\ \lVert y - b\,\mathbf{1} - X w \rVert^2 + \lambda \lVert w \rVert^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b = \bar y - \bar x^\top w , \]

    and substituting \(b\) back leaves exactly the centred problem

    \[ \min_w\ \big\lVert (y - \bar y\,\mathbf{1}) - (X - \mathbf{1}\,\bar x^\top)\, w \big\rVert^2 + \lambda \lVert w \rVert^2 . \]

    So centring a predictor does not change its kernel. When an onset train's zeros become slightly negative under Standardize, that is the intercept being absorbed, not a distortion of the onsets. Where zeros do carry meaning is a nonlinear expansion of event amplitudes, which spline_basis(scope="event") handles.

    Scaling is what matters. With one λ for every predictor, dividing predictor \(j\) by \(s_j\) turns its penalty into \(\lambda / s_j^2\) in its own units. Standardize gives every predictor the same penalty per standard deviation. A kernel then maps one standard deviation of the predictor to one of the target; to read it in original units (µV per onset, say) multiply it by \(\sigma_y / \sigma_x\), both from the training data.

    Raw units¶

    Without scaling, a predictor's penalty depends on its units: a predictor with small variance is penalized more for the same response. In simulation, fitting in raw units silenced the onsets beside the envelope (0.06 of their amplitude) and, with the scales reversed, degraded the envelope instead. Use None only when the units are comparable on purpose.

    Normalize¶

    Min–max scaling subtracts the minimum, not the mean: an offset an intercept would absorb, and TRFlearn has none.

    On the target it breaks the fit. The target's mean, about 0.5, has to come from the predictors, so the kernels take on a large constant component. In simulation the held-out r fell to 0.34 of what the noise allows and the kernels were off by an order of magnitude. target_preprocess="Normalize" is therefore deprecated: it warns, and will be removed.

    On the predictors, with a standardized target, it works, slightly worse than Standardize. Write an uncentred predictor as \(X = X_c + \mathbf{1}\mu^\top\), with \(X_c\) centred. With \(y\) centred too, the cross terms vanish and the loss becomes

    \[ \tfrac{1}{n}\big\lVert y - X_c w - \mathbf{1}\,\mu^\top w \big\rVert^2 = \tfrac{1}{n}\lVert y - X_c w \rVert^2 + (\mu^\top w)^2 , \]

    an extra penalty on each kernel's sum weighted by its predictor's mean. It pulls a predictor with a large mean, such as an envelope, towards a kernel of zero net area. In simulation, both predictors lost 0.05 to 0.14 of their amplitude against Standardize.

    Min–max scaling also takes its scale from the two most extreme samples, so a single artefact sets it. Prefer Standardize.

    Solvers¶

    ridge¶

    The default, and the right one unless a case below applies: it pulls every kernel towards zero, which is what protects a weak predictor.

    Banded ridge¶

    feature_groups gives each group of predictors its own λ (concepts). It pays off when groups differ a lot in how much they explain and each has many columns — acoustic against lexical features, say: the weaker group gets more shrinkage instead of a compromise λ for all. Know its costs:

    • It silences a weak group. Cross-validation only rewards prediction, so a group that adds little gets a very large λ and its kernel goes to almost nothing: in simulation, a weak predictor kept 9 % of its amplitude. Right for a nuisance regressor; wrong when you need that group's TRF.
    • Its λ varies more between subjects, by 0.6 to 1.1 decades in simulation, so a group average mixes kernels shrunk differently.
    • With groups of comparable strength it gains nothing over a single λ.
    • It costs more, by the number of band ratios searched: an 11 × 11 grid on two groups cost 1.1 to 2.2 times a single λ. A random search over ratios (random_bands) samples a few ratios instead of the full product.

    When a band's λ lands on the edge of its grid, the warning names the band.

    ridge-laplacian¶

    Its penalty is the squared difference between neighbouring lags,

    \[ w^\top M w = \sum_d \big(w_{d+1} - w_d\big)^2 , \]

    which charges roughness, not size: a constant kernel costs nothing and a slow one very little. It smooths without shrinking.

    • On a predictor with clear signal it helps: smoother kernels that keep more of their amplitude. In simulation it beat ridge on the envelope beside a weak predictor at every SNR; with two strong predictors it tied from −10 dB up and lost on both at −20 dB.
    • On a weak predictor it fails. Nothing pulls the kernel towards zero, so slow noise enters it as a large, smooth kernel. With 1/f noise, as in EEG, whose power sits in exactly those slow components, the weak onsets' kernel error rose from 1.5 to 5.9 at −20 dB.

    Use it only when every predictor carries clear signal and the SNR is not at the floor.

    Precision and device¶

    float32 gives the same kernels and the same λ as float64, on CPU and GPU. It costs 0.6–0.7× on CPU; on a GPU, 0.2–0.35× of a CPU float64 fit from ten minutes of data up. float64 on a consumer GPU is slower than the CPU.

    Delay margins¶

    delay_margin only matters for a kernel that is still non-zero at the edge of its window. For kernels that decay inside it, it changed nothing in simulation; keep the window wide enough instead.

    Cohorts¶

    TRFGroup costs what a hand-written loop of TRFModel fits does (0.98–1.00×) and returns identical kernels. Asking it for diagnostics or criteria adds 12–19 %, a bootstrap 3–15 %.
